**How to read this file**
- Every claim has its own source. Claim IDs (L1, R1, …) are what the deck's notes and references should point to.
- Source type, strongest first:
  - **[statute]** law text or bill text.
  - **[court]** a court decision or a court-system document.
  - **[publisher]** the journal or working-paper page of the article itself.
  - **[gov]** a government press release.
  - **[secondary]** a law journal, research center, or news outlet.
  - **[data doc]** the UCS files in `docs/`.
- **Status**:
  - ✔ two independent sources agree, or one source of the strongest kind for that claim.
  - ◐ one secondary source only; use with "according to …".
  - ✘ do not use.
- Current statute text: `docs/sources/CPL_510.10_rev2023-06-09.txt` (official text from https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CPL/510.10, revision dated 2023-06-09, copied 2026-11-13). ### 1.3 What this means for our project (our inference, from L5–L9 and our data)

- **Eligibility is mostly set by the charge (L8, L9).** That is one reason why charge and record features alone (`CASE`) reach validation AUC 0.92. Part of what the model learns is the legal rule, not judges' discretion. Say this on the results page.
- **The law changed inside our data window (L4, L5).** Training years (2021–22) straddle May 9, 2022. The validation year (2023) straddles June 2, 2023. The test year (2024) is entirely after both. Put this on the limitations page with the rise in `unseen` judges.
- **$1 bail is about another case (D1 below, L16).** The data dictionary says $1 bail is usually set to credit time served when the person is held on another case, and the statute lets a defendant request nominal bail. Either way it is not a detention decision on this docket. This supports excluding nominal bail (data/README D5) and explains the A1-06 sensitivity result.

How our project differs from R1 (state on the "what already exists" slide): R1 studies 2008–2013, before the 2019 reform, and predicts the defendant's failure to appear. We study 2021–2024, after the reform. We predict the judge's decision itself and measure how much the county and the judge add. Our judge increment is a prediction result. Unlike R1 and R2, we do not use quasi-random assignment, so it is not evidence that judges cause different decisions.
